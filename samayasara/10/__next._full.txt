The *Samayasara* — composed in Prakrit by Acharya Kundakunda — is the most influential philosophical text in the Digambara tradition. Its 415 gathas describe the nature of the soul (*jīva*) — what it truly is, how it becomes entangled in karma, and how it can return to its pure state.

This library presents 12 selected gathas from the Samayasara, chosen to represent the structure of the text — the opening section and eight of its nine chapters (*adhikaras*); the Jīvājīva-adhikāra is not yet represented. The full 415-gatha text is part of a future expansion.

**The opening and the 9 chapters** (gatha numbers as in the *Atmakhyati*):

- **Pūrvaraṅga** (gathas 1–38) — the opening: the invocation, the soul in its own state and in another's, the two standpoints, the pure Knower

1. **Jīvājīva-adhikāra** (gathas 39–68) — the soul distinguished from the non-soul
2. **Kartṛ-karma-adhikāra** (gathas 69–144) — doer and deed: the soul is the doer of its own states, not of material karma
3. **Puṇya-pāpa-adhikāra** (gathas 145–163) — merit and demerit; both bind, despite different effects
4. **Āsrava-adhikāra** (gathas 164–180) — the influx of karma into the soul
5. **Saṁvara-adhikāra** (gathas 181–192) — stoppage of new karmic inflow
6. **Nirjarā-adhikāra** (gathas 193–236) — shedding of accumulated karma
7. **Bandha-adhikāra** (gathas 237–287) — the actual binding of karma
8. **Mokṣa-adhikāra** (gathas 288–307) — liberation, by separating the self from bondage
9. **Sarva-viśuddha-jñāna-adhikāra** (gathas 308–415) — all-pure knowledge: the self as knower, neither doer nor experiencer of karma

Some editions (for example Vijay K. Jain's) count the opening as a chapter of its own, giving ten.

The 12 gathas selected here — 1, 2, 7, 93, 146, 151, 164, 186, 218, 240, 293 and 320 — aim to give a reader a structural sense of the text rather than exhaustive coverage.

**Note on Prakrit:** Samayasara is in Prakrit (*Shauraseni Prakrit*), not Sanskrit. The transliteration uses standard Prakrit conventions; readers familiar with Sanskrit will find some grammar and vocabulary slightly different.

**Important:** Gatha numbers follow Acharya Amritchandra's *Atmakhyati* (415 gathas). Acharya Jayasena's *Tatparyavritti* has 439 gathas and numbers many of the same gathas differently; each verse gives its Tatparyavritti number where it differs. The Prakrit text follows the nikkyjain jainDataBase edition of the Samayasara, which gives both numberings, checked against Vijay K. Jain's 2012 edition; where the two read differently, the verse says so. Reviewer should still verify against an authoritative printed edition before publish.
22:T45d,Acharya Kundakunda (~2nd century CE, sometimes earlier) is the towering figure of Digambara Jain philosophy. His works — especially the *Samayasara*, *Pravachansara*, *Niyamasara*, and *Panchastikaya Sangraha* — are quoted constantly in every later Digambara commentary. Bhattaraka tradition, monastic education, and lay study all return to Kundakunda repeatedly.

Of his works, the *Samayasara* — “the essence of the soul” — is the most influential. In 415 Prakrit gathas (as numbered in Amritchandra's *Atmakhyati* commentary), it lays out the entire Digambara philosophy of the *jīva* (soul): what the soul truly is, how it gets entangled with karma, and how it returns to its pure nature.

Kundakunda is famous for his use of the *niścaya-vyavahāra* distinction — separating the absolute viewpoint (the soul as it really is) from the conventional viewpoint (the soul as it appears entangled in body and karma). This methodological move shapes nearly all subsequent Digambara philosophy.

Almost nothing is known of Kundakunda's historical life. The texts survive; the tradition has carried them.

24:T5c8,The *Bandha-adhikāra* (gathas 237–287) opens with an extended image (237–241). A man rubs his body with oil, stands in a place thick with dust, and exercises with weapons — cutting down palms, plantains and bamboo, damaging living and non-living things. Dust clings to him. Why? This gatha answers: because of the oil, and not because of any of his movements.

Gatha 241 applies the image: in the same way the person of wrong belief, engaged in many kinds of activity and producing attachment and the like in his consciousness (*upayoga*), is smeared with karmic dust. Amṛtacandra's *Ātmakhyāti* argues it out by elimination. The cause of bondage is not the world full of matter fit to become karma — or the Siddhas at the summit of the universe would be bound. It is not activity of body, speech and mind — or ascetics of perfect conduct (*yathākhyāta*) would be bound. It is not the senses — or the omniscient would be bound. It is not harm to living and non-living things — or careful monks observing the *samitis* would be bound. What remains is attachment and the like in consciousness: that alone is the cause of bondage.

The Tattvartha Sutra (6.4) states a related rule in its own terms: the activity of one with passion brings *sāmparāyika* influx, while the activity of one without passion brings only *īryāpatha* influx. For the practitioner the instruction is the same in both texts. The work is not to stop *acting*. It is to remove the oil.
