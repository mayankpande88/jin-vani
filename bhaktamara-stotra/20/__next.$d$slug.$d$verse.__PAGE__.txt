3:T5aa,The Digambara tradition remembers Manatunga as an *Acharya* — a senior monk who had taken the full vows and reached teaching authority within the *sangha*. The Shvetambara tradition also claims him, as Manatunga-suri. We have no surviving biography from his own time. What we know comes from later traditions, which differ in their details; modern scholars place him around the 6th–7th century CE.

What we do have is the **Bhaktamara Stotra itself**. Forty-eight verses in the Digambara recension (forty-four in the Shvetambara) of remarkable Sanskrit poetry, dense with imagery, technically masterful, devotionally profound. The kind of text that suggests deep training in classical Sanskrit poetics combined with deep meditative practice.

The famous legend — bound in 48 chains by a king, freed by 48 verses (44 in Shvetambara accounts) — comes from medieval sources written centuries after him, and varies across traditions. The king most often named, Bhoja, lived centuries after Manatunga. Whether it is historical fact, devotional tradition, or sacred metaphor is something each reader can decide for themselves. What is undeniable: for centuries, this stotra has been recited daily across the Digambara world, as a living tradition.

When you read these verses, you are reading words composed some thirteen or fourteen centuries ago and recited for centuries since. Not in a museum. As living recitation. *That itself is something.*
