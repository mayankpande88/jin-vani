  The Digambara tradition remembers Manatunga as an *Acharya* — a senior monk who had taken the full vows and reached teaching authority within the *sangha*. The Shvetambara tradition also claims him, as Manatunga-suri. We have no surviving biography from his own time. What we know comes from later traditions, which differ in their details; modern scholars place him around the 6th–7th century CE.

  What we do have is the **Bhaktamara Stotra itself**. Forty-eight verses in the Digambara recension (forty-four in the Shvetambara) of remarkable Sanskrit poetry, dense with imagery, technically masterful, devotionally profound. The kind of text that suggests deep training in classical Sanskrit poetics combined with deep meditative practice.

  The famous legend — bound in 48 chains by a king, freed by 48 verses (44 in Shvetambara accounts) — comes from medieval sources written centuries after him, and varies across traditions. The king most often named, Bhoja, lived centuries after Manatunga. Whether it is historical fact, devotional tradition, or sacred metaphor is something each reader can decide for themselves. What is undeniable: for centuries, this stotra has been recited daily across the Digambara world, as a living tradition.

  When you read these verses, you are reading words composed some thirteen or fourteen centuries ago and recited for centuries since. Not in a museum. As living recitation. *That itself is something.*

The Bhaktamara Stotra is one of the most beloved devotional hymns in Jainism, composed in praise of *Bhagavan Adinath* — Rishabhanatha, the first Tirthankara — by Acharya Manatunga. Its 48 verses are recited daily across the Digambara tradition.

Digambara tradition holds that Acharya Manatunga composed the stotra while bound in 48 chains by King Bhoja. With each verse, one chain miraculously broke; by the 48th, he was free. Shvetambara accounts count 44. This library follows the Digambara recension of 48 verses. Verses 32–35, which complete the description of the eight prātihāryas, are absent from the 44-verse Shvetambara recension (many modern scholars regard them as later additions), so from verse 36 onward Digambara verse numbers run four ahead of Shvetambara ones.
