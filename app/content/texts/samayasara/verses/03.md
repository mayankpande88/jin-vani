translation_en: 'From the conventional standpoint, the knowing soul is described as having conduct, faith, and knowledge. (But from the absolute standpoint:) there is no knowledge, no conduct, no faith — only the pure Knower.'

meaning_en: |
  This is one of the most-quoted and most-difficult gathas of the Samayasara (in the main text it is gatha 7). It establishes the famous *vyavahāra–niścaya* distinction that runs throughout Kundakunda&apos;s philosophy.

  *Vyavahāra-naya* — conventional standpoint — describes the soul as *having* faith, knowledge, conduct. This description is useful for teaching, for practice, for everyday speech about the soul.

  *Niścaya-naya* — absolute standpoint — looks deeper. From this view, the soul is not the *bearer* of faith, knowledge, conduct as separate qualities. It is simply *jñāyaka* — the Knower. The three jewels are not three distinct things stuck onto a fourth (the soul). They are the soul, seen from three angles.

  This methodological move shapes most subsequent Digambara philosophy. Don&apos;t stop at the conventional description — go deeper. The absolute reality is more unified than language can comfortably express.

  For a modern reader, this is a profound philosophical claim about how language works. Saying &ldquo;the soul has knowledge&rdquo; is true conventionally — but it suggests two things (soul and knowledge) where there is really one (the knowing-soul). The grammar of subject and predicate misleads us into thinking reality has the same structure.

This is the gatha that, more than any other, defines Kundakunda&apos;s philosophical style. Look once with the conventional eye; look again with the absolute eye. Both are valid. Both are needed. The path begins with the first; it ends with the second.
