Knowledge of the categories comes through *pramāṇa* and *naya*; the chapter has described pramāṇa — the five kinds of knowledge — and now names the nayas. Pramāṇa grasps an object as a whole; a naya grasps one aspect of it. Sarvarthasiddhi says naya is of two basic kinds — *dravyārthika* (concerned with the substance, the general) and *paryāyārthika* (concerned with the mode, the particular) — and then gives the seven:

- **Naigama** — speaks of an intended result as if it were present. A man carrying an axe says he is going to bring a *prastha* (a wooden measure); so far only the intention exists.
- **Saṁgraha** — gathers all members of a class under one word: "existent," "substance," "jar."
- **Vyavahāra** — divides what saṁgraha gathered, step by step: substance into soul and non-soul, soul into celestial, infernal and so on, as far as division can go.
- **Ṛjusūtra** — takes only the present mode, leaving past and future aside.
- **Śabda** — insists that grammar match meaning; it rejects mismatches of gender, number, tense and the like.
- **Samabhirūḍha** — holds that synonyms differ in meaning: *Indra* (the sovereign), *Śakra* (the mighty) and *Purandara* (the destroyer of cities) each name a different aspect.
- **Evaṁbhūta** — applies a word only while the thing is doing what the word says: Indra is "Indra" only while exercising sovereignty.

Each is narrower in scope than the one before. The first four concern the object (*artha-naya*); the last three concern the word (*śabda-naya*).

The key point comes at the end of Pujyapada's commentary: a naya held alone, as if it were the whole truth, is wrong knowledge; held in relation to the others, it is right knowledge. Like threads — separate, they make no cloth; woven together, they do.
